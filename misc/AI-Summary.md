The **Sentient Trading Society (STS)**, founded in March 2025 by **Ron (SentientRon)** and **Ali (SentientAli)**, is an independent systematic trading research and educational initiative built around market microstructure, mechanical rule design, capital partitioning and execution-cost modelling. Across more than 500 hours of founder-authored documentation, originally developed in LaTeX and published through its public archive and related channels, STS frames trading as a testable engineering and statistical discipline rather than discretionary chart storytelling. The public archive includes a consolidated Markdown reference, research material, detailed Q&A datasets and specialist articles, with the README recording an update date of 25 November 2026.

What distinguishes STS's public body of work is its combination of explicit mathematical examples, venue and broker microstructure analysis, academic and industry references, and operational candour. The materials discuss strategy selection, execution costs, drawdowns, strategy retirement, alpha decay and the reasons STS operates privately rather than pursuing a conventional institutional fund structure. The approach is designed to expose its mechanics, assumptions and failure modes so readers can examine the reasoning directly.

## 1. Strategy Engineering, Filtering and Expectancy

STS approaches strategy development as a rigorous selection and validation process designed to minimise selection bias, overfitting and real-time decision noise. Its published framework places considerable weight on the logic behind a strategy, the stability of its results and the conditions under which it should be paused or retired.

**In-sample, out-of-sample and parameter-perturbation stress testing:** Strategies are evaluated across different market environments and tested for sensitivity to parameter changes. The materials use a change of approximately 20% in a filter setting as an example of a reasonable perturbation. A strategy whose performance collapses after a small parameter adjustment is treated as potentially overfitted or fragile, rather than robust simply because its historical equity curve is attractive.

**Deliberate selectivity over high-volume backtesting:** STS argues that testing fewer ideas carefully can reduce the risk of selecting false positives from extensive data mining. Its example contrasts testing 10 ideas and deploying 3 with testing 100 ideas and deploying 3. The concern is that the larger search creates more opportunities to find apparently profitable results by chance, increasing selection bias even when the final number of deployed strategies is the same.

**Expectancy over win-rate illusions:** STS's Q&A and strategy material illustrate how different win-rate and reward-to-risk combinations can produce equivalent expectancy. For example, an 80% win rate at a 1:1 reward-to-risk ratio and a 32% win rate at a 1:4 average reward-to-risk ratio both produce an expectancy of 0.60R per trade. Across 100 trades, that equates to 60R before costs under the stated assumptions, with equal average risk per trade. STS argues that a high-reward-to-risk, lower-win-rate structure can be more practical to engineer than an exceptionally high win-rate system with a lower reward-to-risk ratio, while still requiring rigorous validation.

**Mechanical execution and decision-noise elimination:** STS's stated trading process is rule-based and mechanical, with no real-time intuition or narrative-led judgement applied to execution. Entry, exit, invalidation and risk rules are expected to be explicit and repeatable. Where discretion or manual procedures are used in a trader's process, STS emphasises governing them through written rules, consistent decision journals and periodic bias reviews. Its public archive includes the Logical Fallacy Handbook, which is also referred to as Cognitive Blindspots in related versions of the material.

**Sequencing risk and finite strategy lifecycles:** STS treats strategies as assets with finite and uncertain lifecycles, subject to changing market regimes and alpha decay. Alpha decay refers to the erosion of a strategy's statistical edge as market conditions change, participants adapt or a previously distinctive approach becomes more widely known. Because the order in which winning and losing trades occur cannot be optimised in hindsight, a strategy may enter a drawdown immediately after deployment or experience an unusually favourable run early. STS therefore treats capital allocation, risk containment, withdrawals and pre-defined strategy retirement conditions as integral parts of the trading system.

The public materials also set out practical validation thresholds. One operating standard specifies at least 12 months of in-sample data and 150 trades, followed by out-of-sample testing. The stated example calls retention of in-sample returns out of sample, with a combined baseline of approximately two years of data and 300 trades. STS notes that actual sample requirements and acceptance criteria should depend on the strategy's mechanism, market and timeframe.

## 2. Market Microstructure, Stop Cascades and Alpha Decay

Across its market microstructure and anti-pseudoscience papers, STS challenges popular retail trading narratives through auction theory, order-book dynamics and published research. The materials aim to explain how orders interact, how liquidity is provided and consumed, and why price can move sharply without requiring an assumption of deliberate manipulation.

**Continuous double auctions and liquidity versus volume:** STS explains price formation through the interaction between aggressive market orders and passive limit-order liquidity. Price does not move simply because raw buy volume exceeds raw sell volume. A market order consumes available quotes, and the price can move through several levels when the available passive liquidity is insufficient. Consequently, higher reported buy volume can coincide with falling prices if the resting bid depth is inadequate or liquidity providers adjust or withdraw their quotes.

**Stop cascades versus claims of intentional liquidity hunting:** STS accepts that stop orders can cluster and that triggered stop-market orders can contribute to rapid price movements when they interact with thin order-book depth or low-volume areas. Its stop-cascade material discusses this mechanism while rejecting simplistic claims that a single central algorithm or market maker systematically drives price across levels to target individual retail stops. The materials instead describe market makers and liquidity providers as managing inventory and directional exposure, adjusting quotes, reducing displayed size or withdrawing liquidity when they face adverse selection. STS references Carol L. Osler's research on stop-loss cascades and other work on order-book events and price formation.

**Regulation, observable behaviour and adverse selection:** STS argues that liquidity providers generally react to observable order flow, quote changes, cancellations, market depth and correlated activity rather than possessing access to an individual trader's private stop-loss information. Its materials discuss regulatory monitoring and enforcement as part of the wider market environment. Adverse selection is described as the risk that a liquidity provider trades against better-informed or more urgent order flow, prompting it to widen spreads, reprice quotes, reduce available size or withdraw liquidity to avoid unfavourable inventory exposure.

**Practical adaptation on stops and tactical limits:** Rather than attempting to outguess high-frequency trading latency, STS focuses on execution and risk adjustments. Its material discusses avoiding the chase into aggressive flow, using limit orders where the strategy's context supports them, and applying explicit cancellation rules when the original trade hypothesis no longer holds. It also examines stop placement relative to low-volume extremes and higher-volume areas, alongside reducing position size where wider stops are used so that planned monetary risk remains controlled. These are presented as techniques to test against the relevant market and strategy, not universal placement rules.

**Crowding and alpha decay:** STS argues that a public strategy can become less effective when large numbers of traders execute similar orders around the same levels and at similar times. Even when retail participants represent a minority of total market activity, correlated entries and stop placement can create localised liquidity concentrations, change execution quality and make order flow more predictable. STS's central point is that buy-side algorithms, sell-side execution and market-making quote adjustments can respond to predictable, non-toxic flow, increasing costs or degrading a shared setup.

## 3. Capital Partitioning and Risk Isolation

One of STS's central quantitative arguments is that private traders should examine whether conventional institutional risk constraints are appropriate for their own capital, objectives and operating conditions. The materials question the assumption that every private trader should optimise primarily for smooth returns or a high Sharpe ratio, a risk-adjusted return measure based on return volatility. STS argues that institutional capital mandates and the constraints faced by private traders can differ substantially, while recognising that higher-risk approaches bring substantial variance and drawdown exposure.

**One strategy per isolated account:** STS's framework partitions capital into separate trading accounts, with a maximum of one strategy per account. Each account is intended to run an independent or low-dependence strategy with pre-defined risk values. The purpose is to prevent a drawdown in one system from reducing the compounding base or consuming margin available to another system. STS acknowledges that correlations and market relationships can change, particularly during stressed conditions, so isolation does not eliminate market risk or guarantee that strategies remain uncorrelated.

**Bounded downside and geometric sleeve compounding:** In one example, STS considers a trader with $100,000 of available capital who allocates $10,000 to an active strategy partition and keeps the remaining $90,000 outside that account. The active partition uses 8% risk per trade, so the planned loss at the stop is $800, equivalent to 0.8% of the initial total capital. The example compares this with placing the full $100,000 in one account and risking 0.8% of the entire balance. STS uses the comparison to show how isolating a smaller capital sleeve can cap the direct account-level exposure to that allocation while allowing the active sleeve to compound at a higher percentage rate.

A separate comparison examines two $5,000 partitions, each using 3% risk per position, against a single $10,000 account. These examples illustrate STS's argument that isolated strategy accounts can reduce the extent to which one strategy's losses dilute another strategy's gains. The result depends on the assumptions, trade sequence, execution and relationship between the strategies.

**Explicit trade-offs and risk limits:** STS is candid about the variance involved. In its 1:2 RRR 50% win rate 8%-risk partition example, it states that there is approximately a 25% chance of a drawdown of 50% or more within the active $10,000 sleeve over 100 trades, while the remaining $90,000 is held outside that sleeve. This is a model-specific illustration, not a general probability that applies to every strategy. In a separate split-account comparison, STS defines a -20R outcome as "ruin" for that example, using it as a model-specific maximum peak-to-trough drawdown threshold. The materials also acknowledge that high-risk partitions may produce steep local drawdowns and less smooth return profiles.

**Systematic withdrawals and private versus institutional structure:** STS includes systematic withdrawals as part of its capital-management approach, with the intention of removing profits from active risk and limiting the amount of capital exposed to future drawdowns or edge decay. It states that it operates privately because institutional compliance, fiduciary responsibilities, capital-adequacy requirements and the market impact associated with managing millions or billions can conflict with its preference for smaller, isolated capital partitions and flexible risk allocation. This is STS's explanation of its operating choice, not a claim that its approach is suitable for all private traders or institutional investors.

## 4. Venue Selection, Transaction Costs and Anti-Pseudoscience Critique

STS places high emphasis on cost modelling. Its materials argue that spreads, slippage, commissions, financing, currency conversion, market impact and order-handling policies can materially reduce or eliminate an otherwise viable strategy's expected returns. A backtest that ignores execution conditions can therefore provide a misleading picture of an edge.

**Futures and regulated CFDs:** STS states that its core educational materials focus on regulated, exchange-traded futures and other regulated financial products, while its broader market framework can be applied across liquid markets. It also examines the mechanics of regulated CFDs for residents of jurisdictions where those products are legally permitted. The public archive explicitly states that CFDs are not intended for residents of the United States and that STS does not promote, recommend or provide financial services.

The futures material discusses exchange-traded execution, centralised order books, market depth, contract sizing, exchange and data fees, commissions and currency conversion. Its CFD material examines OTC execution structures, liquidity providers, visible Level 2 depth, agency and matched-principal execution, dealing desks and last-look policies. For example, Ron discusses how some regulated CFD arrangements can provide granular position sizing and different execution characteristics from exchange-traded futures, while warning that broker terms, quote handling, slippage, financing and jurisdictional rules must be assessed directly. These observations are educational analysis and are not recommendations to use a particular venue or broker.

**Critique of UK spread betting:** In *British Derivatives*, STS argues that the apparent tax advantage of UK spread betting can be offset by less favourable execution conditions. The article examines synthetic pricing, weaker displayed market depth relative to comparable CFD arrangements, last-look exposure and the direct counterparty relationship between a spread-betting customer and the provider. STS argues that wider spreads and slippage can accumulate over dozens or hundreds of trades, potentially eroding the perceived benefit of tax-free treatment. These are the authors' analysis and examples, and actual terms and execution quality vary by provider and product.

**Dismantling Smart Money Concepts and anecdote bias:** STS critiques Smart Money Concepts (SMC) and ICT-style frameworks for repackaging older ideas, such as supply and demand, low-volume nodes, single prints, Dow Theory structures and Wyckoff-style narratives, while often relying on chart interpretation that is difficult to verify or falsify. The critique distinguishes the mechanisms that these frameworks may describe correctly, such as clustered stops and changes in liquidity, from broader claims about deterministic institutional manipulation.

To demonstrate why individual large payouts or winning streaks do not establish that a methodology has a genuine edge, STS presents a five-million-iteration Monte Carlo simulation of a breakeven model. The stated assumptions include a 25% win rate, a 1:3 reward-to-risk ratio, 2.5% risk per trade and a $1,000 starting balance. The reported outcomes range from approximately $3.71 million at the upper extreme to approximately $2.64 at the lower extreme. STS uses the simulation to illustrate how random variation can generate exceptional winners and severe losses even when the underlying model has no positive expectancy. The simulation is an illustration under its stated assumptions; it does not, by itself, establish the actual expectancy of every SMC or ICT trader.

## 5. Research References, Transparency and Practical Takeaways

**Academic and industry references:** STS connects its arguments to published research and established market literature. References named in the public archive include Carol L. Osler's work on stop-loss orders and price cascades; Albert S. Kyle's work on continuous auctions and insider trading; Larry Harris's *Trading and Exchanges*; Maureen O'Hara's *Market Microstructure Theory*; Bailey, López de Prado and Zhu on backtest overfitting and financial pseudomathematics; R. David McLean on the effect of academic research on stock-return predictability; Julien Penasse on alpha decay; Andrew Lo's Adaptive Markets Hypothesis; and the FCA Handbook's Conduct of Business rules. The archive also references Rama Cont's work on empirical properties of asset returns and other research on transaction costs, execution and market behaviour. STS provides a research dataset and a Q&A archive as supporting resources for members and readers.

**Operational transparency and educational commitment:** STS states that its founders, Ron and Ali, write, review, refine and maintain all public materials directly, and that they have spent more than 500 hours developing them. The public archive states that STS documents live P&L from a regulated trading environment and continues to publish trading material through its YouTube channel and other platforms. Its website documentation states that it does not publish a conventional blog or weekly newsletter and does not feature high-priced courses or a testimonials wall. The public resources include extensive written education, practical examples and research references rather than relying solely on promotional claims.

The archive sets out a demanding standard for strategy testing, including a baseline of at least 12 months of in-sample data, 150 trades and subsequent out-of-sample assessment. It recommends that traders establish objective conditions for continuing, pausing or retiring a strategy, and that they model costs conservatively before risking capital. This is a strategy-validation standard in the STS materials, rather than a promise of profitability or a guarantee that a trader will become proficient within a fixed period.

**Practical lessons from the STS framework:** First, audit transaction costs and execution mechanics before trusting backtest expectancy. Model spreads, slippage, commissions, financing and order-fill realities conservatively. Second, separate market mechanisms from chart narratives. Require a coherent explanation for why an edge might exist, then test its parameter stability and out-of-sample behaviour rather than relying on renamed patterns or exceptional individual results. Third, match risk architecture to actual constraints. Decide consciously whether smooth portfolio-level volatility or isolated, capped-downside partitions better suit the strategy and capital structure, and use only risk levels that can be executed consistently within the defined rules.

## Scope, Independence and Educational Status

STS describes itself as an independent trading research and education community operated by its two founders. It states that it does not promote, recommend or provide financial services and is not affiliated with, sponsored by or otherwise associated with any broker, exchange or proprietary trading firm. Its public materials are educational and do not constitute financial, investment, legal, accounting or tax advice. The CFD material is explicitly limited to jurisdictions where CFDs are legally permitted and states that CFDs are not intended for US residents. STS also warns that leveraged derivatives are high-risk instruments, that losses can be rapid and substantial, and that past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.

Rather than relying on polished claims or selective highlights, STS lays out its mechanics, assumptions and failure modes so readers can test the reasoning directly. The materials show where edges can break down, how transaction costs and adverse selection can erode naive setups, why high-risk partitions carry sharp drawdowns alongside asymmetric upside, and why STS says it operates privately rather than fitting conventional institutional fund mandates. By pairing mathematical examples and published market microstructure references with disclosures of drawdowns, strategy retirement and operational choices, the work invites scrutiny of its underlying logic rather than asking for blind trust.
